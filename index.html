TimeOmni-o1: Multimodal Time Series Reasoning by Coupling a Time Series Foundation Model with a Large Language Model

A pretrained time series foundation model (TSFM) and a large language model (LLM) connected in a loop: the TSFM lets the LLM read the series, and the LLM's reasoning steers the TSFM's forecast. Trained on MMTR, a multimodal time series reasoning dataset with annotated reasoning traces.

Abstract

Real-world time series rarely come alone: reports, instructions and external events decide what a numerical pattern means and how it will continue. Reading a series together with its context therefore calls for reasoning over both. Existing models cover the task in part. Large language models (LLMs) reason in text but must read a series as digit tokens, which crowd the context window. Time series foundation models (TSFMs) take a series in its native form and forecast unseen series zero-shot, but read no text. Time series language models put a series into an LLM, yet still produce the forecast on the LLM side. The pretrained TSFM’s zero-shot forecasting ability is thereby left unused. We present TimeOmni-o1, which connects a pretrained TSFM and an LLM in a loop. Series features extracted by the TSFM are passed to the LLM, and the LLM’s reasoning is fed back to steer the TSFM’s forecast. One model thereby reasons in text over series and context, and forecasts through the TSFM. We further construct MMTR, a multimodal time series reasoning dataset with annotated reasoning traces: 500k+ samples across 12 understanding disciplines and five event-conditioned forecasting domains. On MMTR, TimeOmni-o1 outperforms external baselines and either of its backbones alone on most disciplines and domains. Training on MMTR strengthens reasoning: the generated traces agree with the annotated ones, and the model surpasses its no-reasoning counterpart where the question requires multi-step counting or fine-grained anomaly discrimination.

Model

TimeOmni-o1 couples a pretrained time series foundation model (TSFM) with a large language model (LLM) so that each does what it was pretrained for: the TSFM encodes the series and produces the forecast, and the LLM writes all text. The two are connected in a loop (Figure 1). For an understanding question the LLM generates a reasoning trace and the answer. For a forecast the trace ends in a forecast instruction, which states the window over which the event acts and the direction, shape and relative magnitude of its effect, and the TSFM forecasts under it.

The loop consists of two pathways (Figure 2). Series to LLM. The TSFM encoder turns the series into patch representations, and a sliding-window Q-Former compresses them into a soft prompt whose size grows only logarithmically with the series length; a few global queries and a statistics token add the global shape and the absolute magnitude that local tokens lack. The LLM reads this soft prompt beside the context text. LLM to TSFM. For forecasting, a feedback Q-Former compresses the LLM's hidden states over the soft prompt, the context and the trace into $M$ tokens, which are injected into every TSFM encoder block through gated cross-attention. The reasoning thus shapes the forecast from inside the forecaster, and the quantile forecast is still produced by the TSFM.

Dataset: MMTR

MMTR is a multimodal time series reasoning dataset in which every sample carries an annotated reasoning trace: 502,586 training and 71,067 test samples over two task types. Among related corpora, only MMTR and the synthetic ST-Bench carry a trace on both task types (Table 1); MMTR does so largely on real series, at twenty times the scale.

Understanding task

497,956 training and 70,630 test samples, unified from six public reasoning-annotated corpora into one schema of series, question, reasoning trace and answer. Answer formats cover open question answering, multiple choice, classification, anomaly detection and diagnosis, and multi-step numerical calculation; series are multivariate where the source provides it. Each sample is assigned to one of 12 disciplines (Table 2).

Forecasting task

4,630 training and 437 test samples, event-conditioned: each pairs a history series with a background and the description of an established event (a heatwave alert, a holiday, a market event) and asks for a forecast over a horizon. Series and dated texts come from five public multimodal forecasting suites, giving ten datasets in five domains (Table 3). Every sample is annotated with an event description, a reasoning trace, a forecast instruction and the event window (ROI) over which the instruction expects the event to act.

Annotation and human review

The forecasting annotator (an LLM) sees the history, the accompanying text and the realized future, but may not cite the future: the trace must follow from the history and the event alone, and the instruction must match what happened. The magnitude each instruction states is calibrated per dataset against the typical peak. Two independent reviewers rated a stratified sample of 1,000 traces against the series and the ground truth: 93.3% of the understanding traces and 97.7% of the forecasting traces pass, and the reviewers agree on 96.6% of the verdicts. Case studies

Six cases show where the reasoning of TimeOmni-o1 decides the answer and where reading the event text decides the forecast, on samples on which the compared models fail. The cases were mined, not hand-picked. On understanding, 29,537 test samples of all subtasks except ECG-QA were screened for those that TimeOmni-o1 answers correctly while the no-reasoning model fails or at most one of nine LLM and external baselines succeeds, which left 965. On forecasting, the 437 test samples were screened for those on which the ROI CRPS of TimeOmni-o1 is below 0.85 times the best of eight competitors, its full-horizon CRPS below 0.9 times, its stated magnitude level matches the annotation and its ROI overlaps the annotated one, which left 39. Three cases per task were then chosen for the clearest mechanism. Baselines are scored leniently throughout: an output counts as correct if the ground-truth phrase appears anywhere in it, a bare option letter counts, and the last number of a numeric answer is taken. A failure is therefore a failure of content, not of format. One boundary holds for every quoted trace. The values a trace cites are approximate, because the LLM reads the series through the soft prompt rather than as digits; what is checked against the raw series is the structural judgment, how many events, how large, and which shape.
